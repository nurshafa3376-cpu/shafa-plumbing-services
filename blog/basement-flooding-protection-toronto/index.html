Basement Flooding Protection in Toronto: Backwater Valves, Sump Pumps, and the City Subsidy

By Shafa Plumbing Services · November 18, 2026 · Serving Toronto & the GTA

One heavy Toronto rainstorm can put inches of water into a finished basement in under an hour — and in older neighbourhoods it happens through the sewer, not the windows. The water comes up through your own floor drain and basement fixtures, carrying whatever was in the city sewer with it.

This article explains the two devices that stop most of it — a backwater valve and a sump pump — and how the City of Toronto's Basement Flooding Protection Subsidy can cover a large share of the cost.

How basements actually flood

There are three common routes:

  1. Sewer backup. During a major storm, the city sewer fills beyond capacity. Pressure pushes sewage back through your home's drain line and out of the lowest openings — floor drains, basement toilets, and laundry tubs.
  2. Groundwater. Rain and melting snow raise the water level around your foundation. Water seeps in through the weeping tiles and foundation, and collects under the basement floor.
  3. Surface water. Poor grading or a blocked drain lets water pool against the house and find its way in.

A backwater valve deals with the first route. A sump pump deals with the second. Many Toronto homes need both.

What a backwater valve does

A backwater valve is a one-way flap installed on your main sewer line, usually in the basement floor near the front of the house. Sewage and drain water flow out of your home normally. If the city sewer surcharges and tries to push water back in, the flap closes and your home is sealed off from the street.

Things worth knowing:

  • The valve has to stay accessible — it sits under a small access box in the floor so it can be opened and cleaned.
  • It needs a quick check once or twice a year: the flap should move freely and be clear of debris and grease.
  • In Toronto, new homes are required to have one. Most older homes — the ones that flood — were built before that rule and have none.

What a sump pump does

Your weeping tiles collect groundwater from around the foundation and drain it into a sump pit in the basement floor. The sump pump sits in that pit and pumps the water up and out, away from the house, before it can rise through the floor.

Things worth knowing:

  • Pumps wear out. A pump that has sat silent for years may fail the first night it is really needed — test yours by pouring a bucket of water into the pit and watching it run.
  • A battery backup keeps the pump working during a power outage — which is exactly when the worst storms hit. This is the single most valuable add-on for most homes.
  • If your pump runs constantly, or the pit overflows, that is a sign the system needs attention, not something to live with.

The City of Toronto subsidy — up to $6,650

Toronto's Basement Flooding Protection Subsidy Program helps homeowners pay for this work. The program was expanded on May 1, 2026, and now covers 80% of the invoiced cost, up to $6,650 per property, across these items:

  • Backwater valve: up to $1,600 per device (maximum 2 devices)
  • Sump pump: up to $2,250
  • Battery backup for the sump pump: up to $300
  • Weeping-tile severance and capping: up to $400
  • Home plumbing assessment: up to $500

A few rules decide whether you actually get the money, and they matter:

  • The work must be done by a contractor holding a valid City of Toronto licence in an eligible category. If the contractor's licence is not in an eligible category, the City can deny the subsidy — so ask any contractor you hire, before work starts, to confirm their licence category qualifies, and get it in writing on the quote.
  • Keep everything: the paid invoice, proof of payment, and any permits. The City processes the claim from paperwork, not from the finished job alone.
  • Apply after the work is done, with the documents the City asks for. Do not assume a verbal "you'll get it back" from anyone — the City decides, not the contractor.

Done properly, the subsidy turns a job many homeowners postpone into one that costs a fraction of a single flood cleanup — and one flood can easily cost more than all of this work combined.

Which homes should act first

This work should move up your list if:

  • Your street or neighbourhood has flooded before — Etobicoke, older parts of Toronto, and low-lying areas near creeks have all seen repeated sewer backups
  • Your basement is finished, or you keep anything valuable down there
  • You have a basement bathroom or laundry — more openings for sewage to come up through
  • Your home was built before backwater valves were required and has never had one installed
  • Your sump pump is old, untested, or has no battery backup
