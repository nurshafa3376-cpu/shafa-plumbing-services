No Hot Water? What to Check Before You Call a Plumber

By Shafa Plumbing Services · November 16, 2026 · Serving Toronto & the GTA

The shower runs cold. Or it starts warm and gives up halfway. Or the whole house has no hot water at all. A water heater problem always seems to happen on the coldest morning of the year — but before you assume the tank is finished, there are a few simple checks worth doing. Some "dead" water heaters are one small setting away from working again.

Here is how to narrow it down safely, what the common causes are, and the signs that mean it is time to call a professional.

First: is it the whole house, or just one tap?

This one question splits the problem in two:

  • No hot water anywhere — the problem is at the water heater itself, or its power or gas supply.
  • Only one shower or tap is lukewarm — the heater is probably fine. The problem is more likely in that fixture: a worn mixing valve or cartridge that is letting cold water blend in.

If it is the whole house, work through the checks below.

Simple checks you can do yourself

  1. Check the temperature setting. The thermostat on the tank should be set around 120°F (49°C). Settings get bumped during cleaning or by curious hands more often than you'd think. If it was turned down, turn it back up and give the tank an hour to recover.
  2. For an electric heater, check the breaker. Find the water heater breaker in your electrical panel. If it has tripped, switch it fully off, then back on. If it trips again right away, stop — that is an electrical fault, not a setting.
  3. For a gas heater, check the pilot light. Look through the small window near the bottom of the tank. If the pilot is out, the tank cannot heat. Many newer units have relighting instructions printed right on the tank — follow those exactly. If you smell gas at any point, do not relight anything: leave the area and call your gas utility or a licensed professional.
  4. Give it time after heavy use. A tank that just filled a big tub and ran two showers needs roughly 30–45 minutes to heat a fresh tankful. If hot water runs out faster than it used to, every time, that is a different problem — see sediment, below.
  5. Look at the tank itself. Puddles underneath, rusty streaks down the side, or rust-coloured hot water are all information. Take a photo — it helps any plumber you call understand what they are walking into.

Why hot water runs out faster than it used to

If the water starts hot but does not last, the usual suspects are:

  • Sediment buildup. Toronto-area water carries minerals that settle at the bottom of the tank over the years. The sediment layer insulates the water from the burner or element, so the heater works harder and heats less. Rumbling or popping sounds from the tank are the classic sign — that is water boiling up through the sediment.
  • A failed dip tube. Cold water enters at the top of the tank and a tube carries it down to the bottom to be heated. When that tube cracks, cold water mixes in at the top — exactly where your hot water is drawn from. The result: showers that turn lukewarm fast, even though the heater still runs.
  • A worn heating element or burner part. Electric tanks have two elements; when the lower one fails, you get roughly half the hot water you should. Gas units can develop burner or thermocouple problems with the same effect.
  • The tank is simply undersized for the house now. More people, a new soaker tub, or a basement apartment can outgrow a tank that was fine before.

The age question

Most tank water heaters last about 8 to 12 years. The age is printed on the rating plate on the tank — look for a serial number or a manufacture date. A heater in that age range that starts needing repairs is usually telling you the honest answer: one repair can make sense, but repeated repairs on an old tank rarely do. A leaking tank cannot be repaired at all — it can only be replaced, and a slow leak left alone can turn into a flood that damages floors and finished basements.

Safety lines that should never be crossed

  • Never ignore a gas smell. Leave, then call for help from outside the home.
  • Never turn the temperature up past 140°F (60°C) to "get more" hot water — that is a scald risk, especially for children and older adults.
  • Never open the tank's panels or touch wiring if you are not qualified. Electric water heaters carry high-voltage power even when they seem off.
  • Treat any active leak seriously. Shut off the cold water supply valve at the top of the tank and call a plumber. If you can do it safely, also switch off the breaker (electric) or set the gas control to pilot.

When it's time to call a plumber

Call a professional when:

  • You have no hot water at all and the checks above did not fix it
  • The breaker keeps tripping, or the pilot will not stay lit
  • You hear rumbling, popping or banging from the tank
  • There is water pooling under or around the tank, or rusty hot water
  • Hot water runs out much faster than it did a year ago
  • The tank is 10 years old or more and starting to act up

A proper diagnosis matters here. "No hot water" can be a thermostat setting, a single part that needs replacing, or a tank at the end of its life — and the right answer is different in each case. The cause gets found first, and the price is explained before any work starts.
